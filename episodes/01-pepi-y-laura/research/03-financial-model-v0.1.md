# Episode 01 — Financial model v0.1 (illustrative, Spain 2026)

**Status:** Working fictional scenario; not a statistical portrait of the average bar, a financial recommendation, or verified local budget.  
**Narrative purpose:** Show complementary contributions, asymmetric capital exposure, financial sustainability and how profit differs from wages. **Do not use the model to assert that any particular allocation is morally justified.**

These are assumed cash uses, not all immediately deductible expenses. Asset ownership, depreciation, inventory and deposits must be separated in an accounting-quality version. The loan's actual personal risk depends on guarantees, company structure and contracts.

## Operational assumptions

- Monthly revenue **excluding VAT**: **€18,000**; 26 trading days, averaging about €692 in net-of-VAT sales per day.
- Food, drinks and consumables: **32% of net sales** (assumption).
- Laura: €24,000/year gross wage, paid on a 12-month equivalent basis (= €2,000/month). Employer cost assumed at **33%** on gross (= €660/month), or €2,660/month total. Actual rates depend on occupation, accident-premium code, contract and other factors.
- Pepi works in the bar: model budgets **€2,700/month total economic cost** for her operating/management labor, distinct from residual profit. This is *not* a personal net wage, and the legal/accounting treatment depends on whether the bar is operated by an autónoma or a company. Use a consistent company-style modeling convention.
- Fixed monthly: rent €1,800; utilities €800; other expenses €1,400; illustrative depreciation €900; illustrative loan interest €200.
- No tax on business profits, owner personal income tax, VAT cash-flow calendar, wage tax withholding, unexpected repairs or changes in working capital yet. Do not present profit as available distributable cash.
- Debt financing **example only**: €40,000 amortizing over 7 years at 6% nominal annual interest (monthly installment approx €584; initial interest €200 and principal approx €384). Loan-principal repayment affects cash, **not profit**.

This is **after recognizing Pepi's labor cost**. A business owner receiving €2,700 for working and €1,780 in residual profit does not simply receive €4,480 net take-home pay. Owner social contributions, tax treatment, corporate distributions and timing require further modeling.

## Sales sensitivity (constant staffing and fixed expenses)

Profit = sales × (1 − 0.32) − €10,460.

| Scenario | Monthly sales | Monthly pre-tax profit |
|---|---:|---:|
| Struggling | 14,000 | -940 |
| Baseline | 18,000 | 1,780 |
| Successful | 22,000 | 4,500 |

These are not measured probabilities or industry margins. The model cannot hold all fixed costs constant for arbitrary sales growth; capacity, hours and staffing eventually constrain it.

**Illustrative break-even net sales** (assuming Pepi is compensated for her labor): €10,460 / 0.68 = **€15,382/month**. This excludes profit taxes and does not show the timing of debt principal or VAT payments.

## Economic and editorial interpretation

- Pepi initially commits personal capital and may bear loan exposure; some investment is assets or cash, not immediately lost.
- Laura initially commits skills, time and dependence on employment earnings; her losses from closure can be severe even without a capital contribution.
- Pepi needs to earn a living **and** seeks a return on her capital, but risk does not establish a unique morally fair profit.
- Different contributions and risks may justify different remuneration. This does not justify *any* wage or profit distribution.
- Profit is contingent and cannot be understood without costs; turnover is not distributable profit.
- Demand from customers is essential; neither the investment nor the employee alone guarantees sustainable jobs.
- An employee may eventually found a firm; cooperatives and independent workers are counterexamples to absolute claims that all jobs require a separate entrepreneur.
- Distinguish economic complementarity from absence of distributional conflicts; collective bargaining, labor law and negotiations can coexist with cooperation.
